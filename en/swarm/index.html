Swarm UAV

A single UAV can do a lot. So why go to the trouble of flying several together? This page explains that, and how the Matris swarm flies at the competition, with a simulation you can play with in every section.

Why UAVs?

A UAV is an aircraft with no pilot on board. It can go where sending a person is dangerous or hard, such as a site after an earthquake, above a forest fire or under a tall bridge. It can also take over wide areas that need surveying the same way every day, like fields, pipelines and coastlines.

Why a swarm rather than one?

One UAV's battery runs out quickly and its camera looks at one spot. When several UAVs share an area the job finishes sooner. If one breaks down the others take over its work. Nor does each vehicle need its own pilot, because the vehicles split a single command among themselves.

What makes a swarm hard?

Vehicles flying a few metres apart must not collide. They all need to hear from each other all the time and know where they are. And it has to be clear who decides, or every vehicle heads its own way.

Avoiding collisions
Each UAV looks at the velocities around it and works out whether a collision is coming within a few seconds (the ORCA algorithm). If so it adjusts its own velocity slightly, otherwise it stays out of the way and keeps flying in its slot.
Communication
The UAVs talk over a Wi-Fi 6 network with UDP packets. The leader sends 20 packets a second and the followers 5. The network is a mesh, so if a UAV drifts out of range its data is relayed through a UAV near it.
Position
GPS tells each UAV where it is. Alongside it, a UWB module measures the distance between UAVs from how long a signal takes to travel. When GPS gets weak, that measurement keeps the formation together.
Decisions
The swarm has a leader and followers. The leader runs the route and the mission, and each follower works out its own position from the leader's. They all run the same software, so if the leader goes down another UAV can take its place.

Technical detail

The area is split into 12 × 8 squares and surveyed in 8 strips. The strips are shared out among the swarm's UAVs in order.

If a UAV goes down, its half-done strip and the strips it had not started go to the UAVs with the least work left. Speeds and times are not real, they are shortened for the simulation.

What does the competition ask for?

At the TEKNOFEST Swarm UAV Competition a swarm of at least three UAVs flies two missions.

Mission 1: Dynamic swarm capability

  • The UAVs take off together in a set formation. Nobody touches a remote from take-off to landing.
  • There are QR codes on the ground. At least one UAV in the swarm reads them with its camera.
  • A code can hold a new formation (line, V or arrowhead), a new altitude or a command to tilt forward, back or sideways. The swarm must keep its centre and spacing while doing it.
  • A code can send one UAV out of the swarm. It lands on a red or blue circle, waits and flies back. If there is a reserve UAV, it can fill the empty place instead.
  • Each code gives the number of the next QR. After the last one the swarm flies back to where it started without breaking formation and lands.

Mission 2: Semi-autonomous swarm control

  • The pilot flies the whole swarm with one remote, as if it were a single UAV.
  • In move mode the swarm goes forward, back and sideways, and climbs or descends. When the pilot turns the swarm, the centre stays put and all the UAVs turn together.
  • In manoeuvre mode the whole formation tilts forward, back or sideways.
  • The swarm must not come apart in any move. If one UAV lags or moves differently from the rest, the mission counts as failed.

Leader, follower and reserve

All Matris UAVs run the same software. Which one leads is settled in flight, and at the start it is the lowest-numbered UAV. Click a UAV to see its role.

Leader

The UAV that knows the route and where the mission stands. It reads the QR code, decides what to do and tells the whole swarm.

  • Runs the route and the order of the mission
  • Reads the QR code and decodes the command in it
  • Sends a state packet of about 500 bytes 20 times a second

Follower

Looks at the leader's packet, works out where it should be in the formation and sends that to its own autopilot. No central computer on the ground or in the air is needed for this.

  • Works out its own place in the formation
  • Corrects its path with ORCA if it gets too close to another UAV
  • Reports its position and state in a packet of about 200 bytes 5 times a second
  • When told to leave the swarm, finds the circle, lands and comes back on its own

Reserve

The UAV waiting on the ground at the starting point. When a place in the swarm empties, the leader sends SUBSTITUTE and the reserve starts its motors and flies there.

  • Waits with its motors off
  • Takes off and flies to the empty place when called
  • Works as a follower once it has joined
Technical detail

The whole swarm's network traffic is about 15 KB a second. The ground station is only used to start the mission, and the swarm makes every decision in flight by itself.

Image processing runs on the UAV, so no video is sent to the ground. Each UAV has a Pixhawk autopilot and a Raspberry Pi 4 mission computer that talk over MAVLink.

QR read

Sample content. The real codes are placed by the referees at the competition.
Technical detail
The leader goes through IDLE, GO_TO_QR, FACE_NORTH, CENTER_QR and EXECUTING in that order. A follower leaving the swarm goes through GO_TO_MARKED, SEARCH_CIRCLE, CENTER_CIRCLE, REJOIN_GROUND and REJOIN_FLYING.
To read a QR code the camera image is scaled to 640×480. Greyscale, thresholding, Gaussian blur and Otsu thresholding are tried in turn and PyZbar decodes the code. The text inside is read as JSON.
The UAV that leaves finds its landing circle by colour (an HSV mask) and by shape (contour analysis).
If the code cannot be read, the swarm holds where it is and slowly descends until it can read it.

If the leader goes down
The leader can leave the swarm in two ways. Either something fails without warning or the mission asks the leader to leave. The swarm reacts differently in each case, so try both.

Technical detail
Each follower counts how long since the leader's last packet. UAV 1 waits 6 seconds and UAV 2 waits 7. The one whose time runs out first declares itself leader, and the other resets its timer when it hears the new leader's packet. That way two leaders never appear at once.
After a failure the new leader takes the swarm home (RTL) and the mission ends there. For the mission to go on, a planned handover (HANDOFF) is needed. The old leader then sends the current mission state to the new leader, which carries on with its own camera.
How does a packet reach a distant UAV?
The leader broadcasts its packet to the whole swarm. If a UAV leaves the leader's range, the packet reaches it through another UAV in between. This is called a mesh network. Drag the UAVs or use the buttons to move them away and watch which path the packet takes.

Technical detail
The network can run on the 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz bands and changes channel when there is interference. The pilot's remote is separate from this network and uses its own 2.4 GHz radio.
A follower that loses its link completely holds its last position and the leader drops it from the active list. When the link comes back the follower returns to the formation by itself.
The range in the simulation is not real, it was chosen so the scene fits the screen.
The geometry of a formation
Each follower knows its place relative to the leader as two numbers, how far ahead and how far to the side. When the swarm turns, both numbers turn by the same angle. Change the heading, spacing and tilt and watch the numbers in the table.

Technical detail
x = cos θ · forward − sin θ · side, y = sin θ · forward + cos θ · side. θ is the swarm's heading, with 0° east and 90° north.
The offsets are (0, ±k·a) for a line, (k·a, ±k·a) for a V and (−k·a, ±k·a) for an arrowhead. k is the slot's rank and a the spacing. Odd slots sit on one side and even slots on the other.
When tilting, each UAV's height difference is its distance from the middle of the formation times tan(angle) · 2.
In the team's code the default spacing and the cruise altitude are both 8 m. Each UAV stays in the same slot for the whole mission.
